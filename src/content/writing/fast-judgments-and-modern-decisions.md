My original essay treated “evolutionary mismatch” as a broad explanation for modern behavior. That is too easy a story to tell. A behavior that looks unhelpful in one setting is not, on that basis alone, evidence that it evolved for a different one. To make that claim well, researchers need a specific mechanism, evidence about how it developed, and a clear account of the environment in which it is being evaluated.

There is a narrower subject with a stronger research footing: people use shortcuts when making judgments, and the way a decision is framed can affect choices under uncertainty. Research on human and nonhuman primate decision-making discusses the origins and limits of observed choice patterns, while also noting that their evolutionary history is not settled by any one experiment ([Santos and Rosati review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4451179/)). The American Psychological Association describes prospect theory as a model of decisions shaped by attitudes toward risk, uncertainty, gains, and losses ([APA Dictionary](https://dictionary.apa.org/prospect-theory)). Neither source says that every poor choice is an ancient adaptation misfiring in modern life.

## A shortcut is not automatically a flaw

A quick judgment can save effort when the available information is incomplete. The same shortcut can mislead when a situation changes, when important evidence is missing, or when the costs of an error are uneven. Whether a shortcut helps depends on the task and its setting. Researchers use terms such as “evolutionary mismatch” for a proposed gap between a trait and an environment, but the concept needs careful specification; it is not a universal explanation for stress, distraction, or bad decisions ([Li, van Vugt, and Colarelli](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0963721417731378)).

I find it more useful to ask what a decision is asking of me. Is it familiar or genuinely new? What information would change my mind? What happens if I am wrong, and can I reverse the choice? Those questions do not diagnose a bias. They create a pause in which I can check assumptions, look for missing alternatives, and seek another perspective when the stakes justify it.

## Keep the explanation proportional to the evidence

“Our brains evolved this way” can sound like an explanation while skipping the evidence needed to support one. A careful account should distinguish a measured decision pattern from a theory about its origin. It should also avoid treating modern life as one uniform environment: a useful shortcut in one context may be a poor fit in another.

That is the scope of this revision. Cognitive shortcuts are real topics of study; an evolutionary account of a particular shortcut is a further, testable proposal. When I make a consequential choice, I can use that distinction as a practical reminder to examine the decision in front of me instead of explaining it with a sweeping story about human nature.
